Abstract

This capstone asks whether information available before a decision date can rank content pages that are likely to experience a meaningful 30-day future decline. Using the pseudonymized FlyRank warehouse, I aggregate a 90-day pre-decision feature window and define decline from the following 30-day impression change. I compare a transparent stale-and-visible baseline with Logistic Regression and Random Forest models using leakage checks, future validation, an unseen-client validation audit, and a sealed May 2026 test period. On the sealed May test, the final Random Forest achieved 0.95 Precision@20, 0.98 Precision@50, 0.852 average precision, and 0.751 ROC-AUC, compared with 0.95, 0.96, 0.675, and 0.500 for the baseline. The final output is a ranked, human-review queue with risk scores, reason codes, and action labels; it is intended as observational decision-support rather than causal evidence about search performance or refresh impact.

1. Introduction / Problem statement

When editorial capacity is limited, the useful question is not simply “which pages are declining?” but which pages should be reviewed first?

A false positive spends limited editorial capacity. A false negative leaves a genuinely declining page lower in the queue. Precision@20 therefore matches the operational top-of-queue decision, while average precision and ROC-AUC describe the quality of the broader ranking.

Careful framing: this is not a prediction of Google’s algorithm and does not prove that a refresh, rewrite, consolidation, or pruning action will cause recovery.

Leakage exclusions

Outcome-derived fields such as trend_direction, trend_pct, future-window metrics, and product decision fields such as health_score, priority_score, and action_type were excluded. Pseudonymous IDs were used only for grouping, joins, and traceability.

3. Methodology

Future-looking target

The target follows a strict temporal shape:

Prior 90-day information → decision date → following 30-day outcome

A page is positive when its future 30-day performance meets the predefined 30% decline threshold, subject to the minimum observation requirements.

5. Limitations & honest framing

  • The data is observational; the model does not establish why a page declines.
  • The target is a defined performance outcome, not direct business impact.
  • The model cannot prove that refreshing, rewriting, consolidating, or pruning causes recovery.
  • The sealed evaluation is one future test period and performance may vary by month, client, content type, and search environment.
  • The panel is unbalanced: clients have different history and tracking coverage.
  • Decline rates changed materially across decision dates, so a single metric should not be treated as timeless.
  • No claim is made about Google’s algorithm or causal refresh impact.

6. Ranked recommendations

The model output is a ranked queue. Each page receives a risk score, rank, reason code, action label, and priority band. Reason codes use only pre-decision features.

Reason codeWhat it signalsSuggested action
TRAFFIC_AND_CLICK_DECLINEImpression and click momentum are both weakPriority refresh review
TRAFFIC_MOMENTUM_WEAKRecent impression momentum is weakPerformance review
CLICK_MOMENTUM_WEAKRecent click momentum is weakCTR/snippet review
LOWER_SEARCH_POSITIONAverage position is relatively weakSearch visibility review
CONTENT_STALENESSContent age is highContent refresh review
MULTI_SIGNAL_REVIEWNo single reason dominatesGeneral decline-risk review

Editorial playbook

  1. Start with the highest-ranked pages.
  2. Inspect the reason code and supporting pre-decision signals.
  3. Check for seasonality, consolidation, tracking changes, and low-volume noise.
  4. Choose the appropriate human action: refresh, performance investigation, CTR/snippet review, search-visibility review, or monitoring.
  5. Keep the editorial decision separate from the model score.
The system prioritizes attention; it does not automatically prescribe an irreversible content action.
